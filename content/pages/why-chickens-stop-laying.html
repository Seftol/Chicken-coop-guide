Why did my chickens stop laying? A checklist with sources

Eight public extension pages name the reasons a backyard hen slows down or stops. This page turns those reasons into a checklist, ordered by how many of the eight pages name each one. Every item says what to check and what that page says to do. Where the pages disagree, both figures stay. They are not averaged into a third figure nobody printed.

This site does not keep chickens. It has not timed a molt, run a light program, or tested a feed. A skipped day is also not, by itself, the problem this list is about. Jacquie Jacob’s page at poultry.extension.org says a hen can lay only one egg in a day, and that an egg takes 26 hours to form, so she lays later and later and then skips a day or more. That is the schedule that page describes. The list below is for a drop that lasts.

How the list is ordered

A cause is counted when one of these eight pages connects it to fewer eggs, to a hen that stops, or to a collection that falls: Virginia Cooperative Extension’s APSC-180 (2022), Oregon State University Extension’s PNW 565 (published January 2003, reviewed 2023), Penn State Extension’s winter-lighting article (updated 9 September 2025), University of Minnesota Extension’s egg page (reviewed in 2025), Minnesota’s cold-weather page (reviewed in 2026), University of Arizona Cooperative Extension’s lighting schedules (January 2026), University of New Hampshire Extension’s “Producing Your Own Eggs”, and Jacob’s page, linked above. The New Hampshire article text does not print a date. Its page metadata records a published time of 9 January 2018 and a modified time of 17 May 2023. Jacob’s page does not print a date.

Day length is on all eight. Molt is on seven. The cold-weather page from Minnesota discusses light and does not use the word molt. Age is on five: Virginia, Oregon, Minnesota’s egg page, New Hampshire, and Jacob. Feed is on four: Virginia, Oregon, New Hampshire, and Jacob, each of which ties the ration to fewer eggs. Water is discussed on those same four. Virginia calls a constant supply a common problem, especially when water freezes. Oregon, New Hampshire, and Jacob connect a lack of water to fewer eggs. Feed is placed first because Virginia and Oregon give nutrition its own heading and discuss water inside nutrition or stress. Disease is on three: Virginia, Oregon, and Jacob. Stress is on three that list it among the reasons laying drops: Virginia, Oregon, and New Hampshire. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says prolonged cold stress can reduce performance, and Penn State says too long a photoperiod causes stress. Those two sentences are in the stress item, and they are not added to the count of three. Parasites are on three: Virginia, New Hampshire, and Jacob. Eggs laid somewhere other than the nest, or eaten, or broken, are on three. Virginia is the page that lists them as reasons the collection falls. New Hampshire describes hiding places and egg eating. Oregon tells keepers to collect from hiding places in its broodiness section. Broodiness itself is on Oregon only.

Those two “most common” sentences are not the same list. Virginia’s most common causes are short days, improper nutrition, disease, advancing age, and negative stress. Oregon’s most common stimuli are decreasing day length, disease, broodiness, poor nutrition, and stress. Broodiness is on Oregon’s sentence and on no other page here, so the count places it last. Age is on Virginia’s sentence and not on Oregon’s, and five pages mention it, so the count places it third. The order below is that count. It is not a survey of flocks.

The checklist

Day length

What to check. How long the day is where the birds live, and whether anyone added light. The calendars below are the ones those pages print. They are northern-hemisphere calendars. South of the equator, including much of Australia, the short days fall around June. In Europe the short days fall in December, and the length still depends on latitude. None of these pages prints a European or Australian day length. Arizona’s page says its own state runs from 9–10 hours in December–January to 14–15 hours in May–July, and that the range still depends on northern versus southern Arizona. Oregon’s page says Oregon goes from nearly 16 hours at the start of summer to just over 8 hours at the start of winter. Use local sunrise and sunset. The arithmetic further down does that subtraction for the times you type.

What the sources say to do. The full lighting section is below. The short version, with the disagreement left intact: Virginia says hens need 14 hours of light to sustain production, and that once day length drops below 12 hours, production decreases and frequently stops. It says that happens naturally from October through February, and it says to hold at least 14 hours, although some recommend at least 16. Oregon says that to maintain production, day length must increase or stay constant at more than 12 hours, and that a 14- to 16-hour day is typical. Penn State says to aim for 16 hours for mature hens, and not to go past that. Minnesota’s egg page says hens need at least 12 to 14 hours a day to keep laying. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says about 14 hours for optimal production. Arizona says 14–16 hours to maintain peak production, and that more than 16 hours does not stimulate more eggs. New Hampshire says a keeper may provide at least 14 hours. Jacob says at least 14 hours to stay in lay year-round, and that the light can be raised slowly to 16 hours late in the laying cycle.

Molt

What to check. Whether feathers are being replaced across the bird, and whether the whole flock dropped feathers at once. Oregon says molt does not cause localized bare spots. Bare spots, that page says, are almost always the result of picking. The same page says rapid feather loss by the entire flock is usually a serious stress, such as lack of water, lack of feed, or a lighting problem. Virginia lists molting among the signs of disease as well as naming it as the rest between laying cycles, so a molt is not always the seasonal one.

What the sources say to do. The pages do not give one length. Oregon says the change in day length causes hens to molt and stop laying, a process that may take several months, and that it is a good idea to let hens molt in their second winter by turning the lights off for about 6 weeks. It also says that a molt during long days is often incomplete, and that those hens may never return to full production. New Hampshire says that if the lights are left off, the days will be short enough by 31 October to stop laying and drop feathers, and that a new set grows in about 6 weeks. Those two dates are the dates that page prints. Penn State says it does not harm the birds to let them molt naturally, and that they return to production in the spring. Arizona says a missed light for even one or two days can trigger a molt that drops production for 6–8 weeks, and that a planned break so hens can molt will reduce laying during that break. Jacob says that if the hens have been laying for 10 months or more, they may be at the end of a cycle: they stop, molt, take a break, and start again. If they have been laying for less than 10 months, that page says to look for something else.

Age

What to check. How old the hen is, whether she has ever laid, and how many months this laying stretch has already lasted. Jacob’s 10-month question is in the molt item above. A hen that has never laid is a different question from a hen that stopped, and the pages do not agree on the starting age.

What the sources say to do. Oregon says a pullet begins at 18 to 20 weeks, peaks at about 35 weeks at a rate greater than 90 percent (9 eggs in 10 days for one hen, or 9 eggs from 10 birds a day), holds that peak for about 10 weeks, and usually produces well until she is 2 to 3 years old. After that, production declines, molts get more frequent and longer, and ovary or oviduct problems can appear. The same page says a commercial Single Comb White Leghorn lays about 265 eggs a year, backyard breeds lay fewer, and more exotic breeds lay more poorly. It also says that after three years a high-producing hen may have laid more than 30 times her body weight.

Minnesota’s egg page says hens begin at around six months, can continue for five to 10 years, peak in the first two years, and lay roughly six eggs a week. Production drops each year when they molt in the early fall and as daylight is lost. New Hampshire says chickens do not start until around four months of age. The same page also says to plan on a five-month growing period, and that five-month-old pullets may be bought ready to lay. Those statements are all on that page. Its example of chicks bought in April or May laying around July or August is that page’s calendar, not a calendar for the southern hemisphere. After the flock is laying, New Hampshire says ten hens should lay about nine eggs a day, easing to six or seven eggs a day after 13 months, and that each hen should produce 20 dozen eggs or more over that period. Twenty dozen is 240 eggs, which is 20 × 12. That multiplication is arithmetic on the figure the page printed.

Virginia says production hens can lay efficiently for two laying cycles, and that after two or three years many decline. It says this varies by bird and by strain. It says good layers lay about 50 to 60 weeks per cycle, then rest in a molt, and that poor layers and old hens molt more often and lay fewer eggs. Jacob says many home flocks produce on and off for three to four years, with fewer eggs each year, larger eggs, and poorer shells. Fifty to 60 weeks, 10 months, 13 months, and two to three years are the units those pages used. This page does not convert them into one age.

New Hampshire also prints yearly estimates for breeds it calls common New England breeds: Barred Rock 200–280 eggs, Black Australorp 200–280, Ameraucana/Easter Eggers 180–200, Buff Orpington 200–280, Rhode Island Red 250–280, Standard Cochin 100–160, and White Leghorn 250–300. Oregon’s figure of about 265 eggs for a commercial Single Comb White Leghorn sits inside that 250–300 range. Jacob says commercial White Leghorns typically do not produce well in home flocks, because they are too flighty, and that commercial-type hens may lay more at first while other breeds tend to lay for more years. Those claims are both printed. They are not combined here.

Arizona says pullets should be on a naturally declining light schedule from 8 to 20 weeks, because laying too early can lead to prolapse and to smaller eggs over the life of the hen. It says to restart lights when pullets are 18 weeks old, and to add only 15–30 minutes a week until 14 hours. Jacob says that if pullets come into lay too early they may prolapse and may lay smaller eggs for the whole cycle, and that light should be increased slowly to about 14 hours when they are ready to lay. Early laying is a management problem those two pages describe. It is not the same event as a mature hen stopping in winter.

Oregon describes three failures in older hens that stop production: an egg trapped in the oviduct (egg bound), yolks that miss the oviduct and stay in the body cavity (an internal layer, which that page says is a problem when it happens every day), and prolapse after a very large egg or a double-yolked egg. It says other hens will pick at prolapsed tissue, so remove that hen from the flock immediately, and that little can be done for these hens except to remove them. Virginia, in its nutrition section, says a vent prolapse usually causes permanent damage and is fatal in many cases. Neither page prints a home treatment. This page does not add one.

Feed

What to check. Whether the feed is a layer ration, whether the birds can eat it free choice, and how much scratch, corn, or table scrap is going in beside it. Also whether a young bird is on layer feed, or a laying hen is still on starter or grower.

What the sources say to do. Virginia says layers need continuous access to a balanced layer food with 16%–18% protein, and oyster shell freely available. It says treats, whole grains, scratch, and table scraps in too large an amount can unbalance the diet, and that those imbalances can cause a vent prolapse. It does not print a gram limit for treats. Oregon does not print a protein percentage. It says to feed a layer ration free choice, to offer oyster shell free choice in a separate feeder, and to supplement only what the hens clean up in 15 minutes, or nothing. It says not to feed starter or grower diets to layers: the nutrient levels differ, and medications in starter diets may leave residues in eggs. New Hampshire says a complete laying ration is about 16%–18% crude protein and 4% calcium. It says not to feed extra cracked corn, because that can unbalance the ration, add visceral fat, and interfere with laying. It says birds eat about 1/2 cup of feed a day, depending on size. Jacob says not to dilute a complete feed with scratch grains, because that dilutes nutrients and can push hens into a molt and out of production. It says not to feed a meat-maker diet to pullets or layers, and not to feed a layer diet to growing chickens, because the calcium level that shells need is harmful to birds that are not laying. It says some hens need more calcium than others, and that oyster shell is an additional source. Minnesota’s egg page says to feed a prepared balanced feed, with crushed oyster shell for eggs and grit for digestion, and that scratch is fine in moderation because it does not supply the whole diet. It does not print a protein percentage. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says a balanced diet matters for feathers and eggs, that feed intake may rise by up to 25 percent in cold weather, and that scratch should stay at no more than a handful per 10 birds. Treats, that page says, should never replace the ration. The 15-minute limit, the handful, and “not too large an amount” are three different instructions. They are not averaged here.

Water

What to check. Whether water is present, liquid, and being drunk. In cold weather, check for ice. In hot weather, check whether the water has sat in the sun.

What the sources say to do. Virginia calls a constant supply of fresh water a common problem in this discussion, especially in the coldest months when water freezes. Oregon lists lack of water as a stress that can stop production, and as one cause of a sudden whole-flock feather drop. New Hampshire says one day without water, for any reason, means less egg production over the next few days, because eggs are 75 percent water. The same page says birds drink more in winter if the water is 50°F, and drink 25 percent more when temperatures go over 80°F. 50°F is 10°C, because (50 − 32) × 5/9 = 10. 80°F is 26.7°C when (80 − 32) × 5/9 is rounded to one decimal place. Jacob says hens will not eat if they cannot drink, so a frozen waterer stops feed intake as well, and that summer water can get warm enough that hens do not drink what they need. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says that without water, chickens stop eating, and it tells keepers to keep water from freezing. Its egg page says continual fresh water matters, especially in summer when hens pant to cool themselves.

Disease

What to check. Whether the drop arrived with sick birds, or with shells that changed first. The signs the pages name, and when they say to call a veterinarian, are in the short section below. Jacob asks two questions before that: did shell quality fail before the eggs stopped, and has the flock already been sick. It says several diseases can produce abnormal shells, and that a flock which has been sick will not perform as well as one which has not.

What the sources say to do. Virginia’s prevention list, in the same disease section, is designated shoes, disinfecting between flocks, washing hands, buying chicks or eggs from a hatchery in the National Poultry Improvement Plan, and isolating new adult birds for a few weeks. It says adult birds can look healthy and still carry disease. The veterinary instruction itself is the section linked above. This page does not add a treatment.

Stress

What to check. A recent move, handling, a new bird or a new rooster, fright, heat, cold and damp, crowding, or a predator visiting. Oregon’s list of stresses that can stop laying, even when they are easy to miss, is moving, handling, overheating, fright, and lack of food or water. Virginia’s list is chilling in damp, drafty conditions, handling or moving, a change in the pecking order, parasites, fright, and predators. It names birds of prey, raccoons, snakes, and other predators, and it says their presence alone can cut production. New Hampshire lists bad weather, severe cold, and predators among the conditions that may reduce laying. Crowding, on that page, is grouped with boredom, excessive heat, and light that is too bright as stresses that can lead to pecking or to eating eggs. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says prolonged cold stress can reduce performance. Penn State says a photoperiod that is too long stresses birds: they get restless and flighty and may peck.

What the sources say to do. Oregon says protection from weather and predators, clean housing, ventilation in a closed house, and feed and water that do not run out. Virginia says to limit unnecessary moving and handling once the flock is in place, to limit children, dogs, livestock, vehicles, and loud noise around the birds, and to keep them out of wet drafts. On chilling, Virginia says access to a heat lamp lets hens regulate their own access to heat. Arizona says heat lamps carry a large risk of fire and should be avoided, and that a heat lamp is the wrong bulb for an egg-laying light. Those two instructions conflict. The fire warnings are quoted in the bulb section, from the pages that print them. For crowding and for how many nest boxes the housing pages ask for, use the space guide. This page does not repeat those floor or nest counts. Jacob names space as one of the variables that change how many eggs a flock lays, and for how many years. The figures are on that guide.

Parasites

What to check. External parasites and internal parasites. Jacob says a heavy internal infestation can damage the digestive tract and cut performance, and that a heavy mite infestation can cause anemia. New Hampshire says to check a few birds once a month, especially around the vent under the tail. It says mites are carried by wild birds and can lower egg production or kill birds if they are not controlled. Minnesota’s egg page says dust baths help control mites, and that a fall clean helps mite control over winter. It does not say mites are why a flock stopped.

What the sources say to do. Virginia says to get a proper diagnosis and treatment. New Hampshire says most farm supply stores carry pesticides registered for poultry parasites, and that the label has to be read and followed. It also says daily access to loose soil, sand, or bare ground lets hens dust bathe, which helps with parasites. Jacob does not name a product. This page does not name a dose.

Eggs that are not in the nest

What to check. Hiding places, broken eggs, and whether hens or a predator are eating them. A lower basket can be a collection problem while the hens are still laying. Virginia lists four of these: predators and snakes eating eggs, hens eating eggs, excessive breakage, and hens hiding eggs when they can range. New Hampshire says hens allowed to roam may lay under buildings or out in the bushes. Oregon, in its broodiness section, says to collect eggs every day from nests and from hiding places.

What the sources say to do. Collect often enough that eggs do not pile up. Oregon says daily. Minnesota’s egg page says twice a day. New Hampshire says at least twice a day, notes that laying is generally in the morning, and says there is less breakage and dirt if eggs are gathered at noon. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says most chickens finish before noon, to collect as soon as you can, and to discard eggs with cracked shells. On egg eating, New Hampshire says to check overcrowding and lighting that is too bright, and it suggests darkening the pen or giving the birds something else to peck. The things it names are salad greens, pine branches, hanging cabbages, and a bale of hay with seed in it. Virginia lists egg eating and does not print a fix. Nest-box counts are on the space guide, not restated here. Arizona adds one lighting detail that belongs with nests: nesting areas should stay dim, and a curtain over the boxes can keep them that way, because hens prefer a darker, secluded place to lay.

Broodiness

What to check. A hen who stays on the nest and will not get off. Only the Oregon page, among these eight, treats broodiness as a standard reason laying stops. It says most hens eventually go broody, some breeds more than others. It names Cochins and Silkies as the breeds that do it most, and Leghorns as a breed in which broodiness is rare. It says the usual trigger is a nest full of eggs, and that some hens go broody without that trigger. Hormonal changes then stop lay.

What the sources say to do. Oregon says to collect eggs daily from nests and hiding places. If a hen stays on the nest for long stretches, remove her from the nest for several days. It says the behavior then stops and she returns to laying. No other page in this set prints that instruction.

Supplemental winter lighting

Short days are the item every one of these pages names. Virginia and Oregon describe artificial light as what prevents that loss. Penn State, Minnesota, Arizona, and New Hampshire tie the same tool to a goal of eggs through the short days, and several of them also describe a rest. The dates in this section are the dates each page prints for its own region. They are not a single calendar for the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, and Europe together.

A rest, and what extra light is for

Penn State’s reason to leave the lights off is stated in one sentence: it does not harm the birds to let them molt naturally. They return to production in the spring. The same page says that if year-round eggs are the goal, artificial light is one of the best ways to support the hens, and that 16 hours simulates the summer photoperiod, which that page calls ideal for supporting peak production.

New Hampshire says providing at least 14 hours of light from 1 September to 1 May, with a 25-watt bulb, will keep hens laying through the shorter days, and that this interferes with the natural molt. It says feathers are how the birds cool themselves in summer and insulate themselves in winter, and that molting is how they replace that set. If the lights stay off, that page says, by 31 October the days are short enough to stop laying and drop feathers, and in about 6 weeks a new set has grown. Light can then be brought back slowly. Those dates are New Hampshire’s.

Oregon says preventing the loss from a shortening day requires artificial light. It also says hens eventually molt even on a lighting program, and that a molt in long days is often incomplete, so those hens may never get back to full production. Its own compromise is to turn the lights off for about 6 weeks in the second winter, let the molt finish, and then put the birds back on long days. A planned break of about 6 weeks, and a one-day lapse, are both on that page. The one-day lapse, it says, can hurt production, so a program that has been started has to be continued. The six-week break is the exception it prints.

Arizona says the winter drop is a light effect. Cold, it says, is the wrong explanation. Used with adequate nutrition, including calcium, and kept at 14–16 hours rather than left on all night, supplemental light does not harm hens and does not shorten the laying life. The page says hens are born with many more egg follicles than they lay in a normal lifetime, so a steady photoperiod supports a cycle rather than using up a limited supply. It also says lights can be discontinued when natural day length matches the photoperiod, and started again when natural daylight falls below 14 hours. Forgetting the lights for one or two days, it says, can drop production for a while or trigger a molt that drops production for 6–8 weeks.

Virginia’s instruction is to add light so the natural October-through-February stop does not happen. Minnesota’s egg page says production falls in the early-fall molt and as daylight is lost, and that a regular bulb can supply the 12 to 14 hours it names. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says to add light as the days shorten if the goal is to encourage laying, at about 14 hours.

Hours, time of day, and how fast to change

The hour figures, stated again so the disagreement is in one place:

  • Virginia: 14 hours to sustain lay. Below 12 hours, production decreases and frequently stops. Hold at least 14. Some recommend at least 16. Allow at least 6 hours of continuous dark. Do not leave lights on continuously.
  • Oregon: more than 12 hours, increasing or held constant, to maintain lay. A 14- to 16-hour day is typical. A program, once started, continues. Even a one-day lapse can hurt. The second-winter exception is about 6 weeks with the lights off.
  • Penn State: 16 hours for mature hens. Do not go over that. Change the photoperiod by no more than 1 hour a week. In the United States, the page says, the drop and the molt come most often in September and October, when day length reaches and goes below 12 hours.
  • Minnesota, egg page: at least 12 to 14 hours.
  • Minnesota, cold-weather page: about 14 hours. Add the extra light in the morning, before sunrise. After 20 December, that page says, daylight increases, and the timer has to be adjusted so the total stays about 14 hours. 20 December is the date that page prints.
  • Arizona: 14–16 hours. More than 16 hours does not add more eggs. Do not leave lights on 24 hours. Change the schedule by 15–30 minutes a week. Adjust every couple of weeks as sunrise and sunset move.
  • New Hampshire: at least 14 hours, in the 1 September to 1 May window that page prints, knowing it interferes with molt. Bring light back slowly after the molt. It does not print a minutes-per-week rate.
  • Jacob: at least 14 hours to hold lay, raised slowly to 16 hours late in the cycle. No minutes-per-week rate. For chicks, the same page says 23 to 24 hours for the first few days so they can find feed and water, then fewer hours. Indoors, that can be 8 hours, until the pullets are ready to lay and the light is raised slowly to about 14.

Penn State’s cap of 1 hour a week and Arizona’s 15–30 minutes a week are the two rates these pages print. This page does not pick a rate between them. New Hampshire’s “slowly” and Jacob’s “slowly” do not add a third number.

Morning versus evening is also a split. Virginia says to add light in the morning so birds go to roost with the sunset. If lights must go off after dark, it says, dim them first, or birds may be stranded, unable to find the perch. Penn State’s worked example is the same shape: artificial light in the early morning, then the natural sunset sends the birds in. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says chickens cannot see well in the dark, so light added after sunset can leave them on the floor all night when the bulb goes off. Arizona says the lighting should ideally all be before sunrise, for the same roosting reason. Its article text says that in the darkest weeks the lights need to come on as early as 3:20 a.m. The Flagstaff table on that page prints 3:05 a.m. for the weeks of 2 December and 16 December. Both are on the page. They are Arizona clock times, for Tucson and Flagstaff, not a universal alarm. If some or all of the extra light is after sunset, Arizona says to dim it over 30–45 minutes. Jacob describes a timer that turns the light on before sunrise and again in the evening before sunset, so the total reaches 14 to 16 hours. That evening block is what the Virginia, Penn State, Minnesota, and Arizona pages are trying to avoid, unless it is dimmed.

One Arizona row is exact subtraction, which is the arithmetic the helper below uses. For the week of 4 November, the Tucson table prints sunrise 6:40 a.m., sunset 5:20 p.m., 10 hours 40 minutes of daylight, and lights on at 3:20 a.m. for a 14-hour day. 6:40 a.m. minus (14 hours minus 10 hours 40 minutes) is 3:20 a.m. Other rows on the Tucson and Flagstaff tables are rounded. The full schedules are on Arizona’s page. They are not copied here, and they are not a schedule for any other town.

Oregon says the light only needs to be bright enough to read a newspaper, that the bulb type does not matter, and that it is best to use an inexpensive timer. Virginia says a timer could help. Penn State says to use timers. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says a regular lamp and a timer. Arizona says to use an automatic timer and to check it. Jacob says to use a light with a stop and start timer.

Bulbs, brightness, and fire risk

The bulb figures are not one specification.

  • Oregon: bright enough to read a newspaper. Bulb type does not matter.
  • Virginia: one 4–5 watt LED (40 watt equivalent) for each 100 square feet of coop. Warm-white or yellow, to imitate sunlight. 100 square feet × 0.09290304 = 9.290304, which is 9.29 square metres to two decimal places.
  • Penn State: orange and red wavelengths. A warm bulb rated under 3500K. A daylight or white bulb also works if it is not too green or blue. Hens need a minimum of 1/2 foot-candle (5 lux). Bulbs only up to 30–40 watts, because brighter light can cause stress and pecking, and still bright enough for a person to see every part of the coop and look birds over for injuries. Incandescent, LED, and other types are all fine if color and brightness are right. The parenthetical on that page is “not heat lamps.”
  • Arizona: one 60-watt incandescent, or a 9-watt LED of similar brightness, per 200 square feet of coop and run. Warm, red-orange wavelengths rather than cool light. Keep bulbs clean, hang them so birds and moisture cannot disturb them, and keep nests dim. 200 square feet × 0.09290304 = 18.580608, which is 18.58 square metres to two decimal places. A heat-producing bulb is unnecessary, that page says, if the coop already protects the birds, and it should not be the light used to raise egg production.
  • New Hampshire: a 25-watt bulb for the 14-hour winter window it describes. Light that is too bright is one of the stresses it links to pecking and egg eating.
  • Jacob: a typical 60-watt incandescent is enough for a small laying flock, and the light does not have to be overly bright. A sensor can keep the bulb off when daylight is already available.

On fire, the pages that give a warning are these. Penn State says to be aware of fire risks with any electrical wiring and devices in the coop, to check lights and wiring for dust, and to place them where birds cannot jump on them or peck them. Arizona says heat lamps carry a large risk of fire and should be avoided. If the coop needs heat, that page points to a heating panel instead. Minnesota’s cold-weather page, writing about heat rather than about an egg light, says never to hang a heat lamp by its cord, to use a cage around the bulb so a fall does not put the bulb in the bedding, to keep wires away from poultry, water, and flammable litter, to use extension cords only in the short term, and not to power the coop with one. It says to use a professional electrician when electricity is installed in the coop. The same wiring sentences appear again in that page’s watering section. Virginia’s heat-lamp sentence, under chilling, does not include a fire warning. It is the sentence quoted in the stress item above.

Morning switch-on time, as arithmetic

This form subtracts clock times. It does not look up a town, it does not know which hemisphere you are in, and it does not decide whether a flock should be lit. You type today’s sunrise and today’s sunset, and you pick a target that one or more of the pages actually print: 12 hours, 14 hours, or 16 hours. Twelve hours is only Minnesota’s low end (“at least 12 to 14”). Virginia says production decreases and frequently stops below 12 hours, and Oregon says maintenance needs more than 12. Fourteen hours is the figure Virginia, Minnesota’s cold-weather page, New Hampshire, and Jacob’s minimum all print, and it is the bottom of Oregon’s typical 14- to 16-hour day and of Arizona’s 14–16. Sixteen hours is Penn State’s aim for mature hens, the top of that Oregon and Arizona range, the “some recommend at least 16” line on Virginia’s page, and Jacob’s late-cycle figure.

The subtraction is the morning-only method: extra minutes are taken off sunrise, and sunset is left alone. That is the method Virginia, Penn State’s example, Minnesota’s cold-weather page, and Arizona’s preferred schedule describe. It is not Jacob’s morning-plus-evening timer. If the day you type is already as long as the target, the result is no added morning light for that target. Arizona’s published Tucson and Flagstaff times are rounded on some rows, so this subtraction will not match every cell of those tables. The 4 November Tucson row cited above does match.

The clock time where the birds are. The form does not convert time zones.

When to call a veterinarian

Virginia says a drop in egg production is often one of the first signs of disease. The other signs it names are a dull, listless appearance, watery eyes and nostrils, coughing, molting, lameness, and deaths. It says some death over a year can be normal in any flock. If you suspect disease, it says to contact a skilled veterinarian, and that home remedies are “not often in the animal’s best interest.” It points readers to the Association of Avian Veterinarians to find a specialist. Oregon says that in many cases a drop in egg production is the first sign of trouble, and it names lethargy, inactivity, lameness, coughing, a dull appearance, and death. It says some mortality is normal, and that if several birds show similar symptoms, seek professional help. For keepers in Oregon, Washington, and Idaho, that page lists diagnostic labs and phone numbers. Those numbers are for those three states. New Hampshire says to consult a veterinarian if other hens look sickly. Jacob does not itself say to call a veterinarian. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says to consult a veterinarian for frostbite or an infection. That sentence is about frostbite, which the page ties to moisture and cold, not to a winter pause in laying.

What this page does not decide

It does not choose 14 hours over 16, or a morning light over a natural molt. Both are printed. It does not turn Oregon’s nearly 16 hours and just over 8 hours, or Arizona’s 9–10 and 14–15, into a day length for the United Kingdom, the European Union, or Australia. It does not size a coop. Floor area, roost length, and nest-box counts are on the space guide. It does not say whether a city allows poultry. New Hampshire says to consult local zoning before keeping birds. That is a local rule, and it is not restated here as if it applied anywhere else.

It is not a diagnosis. Egg binding, prolapse, and disease are described only in the words the pages use. Nothing on this page is a dose, a prescription, or a claim that a particular hen’s eggs stopped for one reason.
