How many nesting boxes do chickens need?

The pages that print a nest-box count do not print the same one. Oregon State University Extension says about one box for every four birds. The University of Florida, and a small-flock fact sheet from New Hampshire and Connecticut, say one box for every five hens. An Ask Extension answer says one for every four or five hens, and at least two boxes even for a small flock. A separate egg-eating page says never to provide fewer than six boxes. The RSPCA’s July 2025 welfare standards for laying hens require not less than one individual box per five hens, and that document is a commercial farm standard.

This page lines those counts up for flocks of 2 to 20 hens, then the box sizes and mounting heights the same pages print. Inches are converted to centimetres by multiplying by 2.54. Where that product is exact, it is given as exact. The ratios are not averaged into a third ratio nobody printed. This site does not keep chickens, and it has not built or measured a nest box.

Short answer. For a backyard laying flock, the extension figures in the table are one box per four hens or one box per five, rounded up to a whole box, with a floor of two boxes from the Ask Extension answer. A 12 by 12 by 12 inch box, 30.48 centimetres on each side, is the size most of those pages print. Mounting height runs from “not on the ground” to about two feet, which is 60.96 centimetres, and the pages do not agree. Hens piling into one box, and hens sleeping in the boxes, have separate instructions further down.

Boxes for 2 to 20 hens

Each cell is division, then rounding up. Hen count divided by 4, or by 5, is raised to the next whole box when the result is not already whole. For 6 hens, 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5, which rounds up to 2, and 6 ÷ 5 = 1.2, which rounds up to 2. For 4 hens, 4 ÷ 4 = 1, and 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8, which rounds up to 1. The number before a parenthesis is that ratio by itself. The 2 inside the parenthesis is the same cell after the Ask Extension minimum of two boxes. Where the cell is a single number, the ratio already came out at 2 or more, so the minimum did not change it.

The one-per-four column is Oregon State University Extension, EC 1644 (published April 2014, reviewed 2023), which says one nest box for about every four birds. The one-per-five column uses the same arithmetic on three pages that print one box per five hens: University of Florida IFAS, AN239 (original publication April 2010, revised November 2019 and November 2023), the University of New Hampshire and University of Connecticut small-flock fact sheet dated 8/08, and the individual-box clause in the RSPCA welfare standards for laying hens (July 2025). Florida and the fact sheet are backyard publications. The RSPCA standard is the commercial scheme described under the ratios. It shares this column because its individual-box ratio is the same. The ratios section keeps that document labeled as a commercial farm standard.

Two other printed rules are left out of the columns on purpose. Ask Extension #843851 (answered 4 August 2023) says one box for every 4 or 5 hens, so it spans both columns, and it adds the minimum of two. University of Maryland Extension (updated 15 September 2021) says one box for every four or five hens, which is the same span, and it is not placed in either column. Jacquie Jacob’s egg-eating page says one 12-inch-by-12-inch nest for every four or five hens, “never using fewer than six nesting boxes.” Every cell above is under six. Using that sentence as a floor would replace the whole table with 6. The table does not do that. The sentence is a different instruction, and it stays in the next section.

Floor area, roost length, and a wider set of nest-count rules from pages that are not repeated here are on the space guide. How many hens a household needs for the eggs it eats is a separate question, on how many chickens for eggs.

Where the ratios come from

Oregon State’s EC 1644, “Living on the land: Backyard chicken coop design,” by Dani Annala, Brian Tuck, Susan Kerr, Ellen Hammond, and Shilah Olson, says to build one nest box for about every four birds. The word on that page is “about.” The table still uses one per four and rounds up, and it labels that step as arithmetic.

Florida’s AN239, “Raising Backyard Chickens for Eggs,” by Mary E. Henry, Jessica M. Ryals, Alicia Halbritter, and Derek L. Barber, says to provide at least one nest box for every five hens. It also says the boxes should be in place as the birds reach 18 to 20 weeks.

The small-flock fact sheet is the Disease sheet in the Small Flock Poultry Management Series, by Tina Savage of the University of New Hampshire Cooperative Extension in collaboration with Michael J. Darre of the University of Connecticut. The sheet prints the date 8/08. The nest sentence sits in a list of ways to limit feather-pecking and cannibalism, after a note that vent-picking is more prevalent when birds lay on the floor in overcrowded systems. It says to provide one nest box for every five hens, and that too many is better than not enough. The “again” in that sentence points back to the previous bullet, which says a little too much feeder and drinker space is better than not enough. The nest line is part of that prevention list.

Ask Extension #843851 is a question from Gila County, Arizona, asked on 2 August 2023: how to get chickens to lay in one spot only. The expert response, dated 4 August 2023 and signed “An Ask Extension Expert,” does not print a person’s name. It says it is usually recommended to have one nest box for every 4 or 5 hens, but at least two nest boxes even for a small flock. The answer does not say the numbers apply only in that county.

Maryland’s “Production - Specific Requirements” page, updated 15 September 2021, does not print an author. In the layers section it says one 10 by 10 inch nest box for every four or five hens. Ten inches is 25.4 centimetres. The page also says to collect eggs twice a day. The size is the part that differs from the 12-inch cube, and it is in the size section.

The RSPCA welfare standards for laying hens (July 2025) open by describing the document as the only RSPCA-approved scheme for rearing, handling, transport, and slaughter of laying hens. They take account of UK legislation, government welfare codes, scientific research, veterinary advice, the Animal Welfare Committee, and farming-industry experience. The Farm Animals Department section says the standards are also achievable on commercial farms. Standard E 9.1 says nest boxes must be provided at not less than 1 per 5 hens if they are individual boxes, or 1 square metre of nesting substrate per 120 hens for group nesting, and that the boxes must be enclosed and draught-free. One square metre is 10.76 square feet when the result of dividing 1 by 0.09290304 is rounded to two decimal places. The group-nesting clause is the other way that standard allows a farm to meet E 9.1. This page does not divide 1 square metre by 120 and offer the result to a backyard flock of six. The individual-box ratio is the figure in the table, and it is labeled there as a commercial standard.

Jacob’s page, “Egg eating by chickens in small and backyard flocks,” is about preventing and stopping egg eating in small and backyard flocks. The page does not print a date. Under prevention it says to provide one 12-inch-by-12-inch nest for every four or five hens, “never using fewer than six nesting boxes.” For a flock of 2 to 20, both columns in the table stay below that floor of six. The two instructions are both printed. This page does not pick a number between 2 and 6.

Kansas State University’s EP-70, “Preventing Hens From Eating Eggs,” by R. Scott Beyer (March 2000), is also an egg-eating bulletin. It says to provide at least one 12 by 12 inch nest for every 4 or 5 hens. What it says about darkness, bedding, and collection is in the sections below. The copy read for this page is the PDF served from the Kansas State University bookstore.

How big a nest box is

A 12-inch cube is the figure that shows up most often. Twelve inches times 2.54 is 30.48 centimetres, so a 12 by 12 by 12 inch box is 30.48 by 30.48 by 30.48 centimetres.

  • Oregon State says each box should be about 12 inches deep by 12 inches wide by 12 inches tall. The “about” is in that sentence.
  • Florida says boxes that measure 12 by 12 by 12 inches and are half filled with straw or wood shavings are ideal. “Half filled” is the fill it prints. It does not print a depth in inches for the straw.
  • The New Hampshire and Connecticut fact sheet says nest boxes should be 12 by 12 by 12 inches, fairly private and dark inside.
  • Ask Extension says that for standard-sized breeds a 12 by 12 by 12 inch cube usually works well, and that a keeper may want a larger box for Jersey Giants or Brahmas. It does not print the larger measurement.
  • Jacob’s page states the floor of the box as 12 inches by 12 inches. It does not state a height.
  • Kansas State states a 12 by 12 inch nest. It does not state a height.
  • Maryland’s box is the one that differs: 10 by 10 inches, which is 25.4 by 25.4 centimetres. It does not state a height.

No university page in this set prints a separate bantam measurement, or a measurement for a heavy breed. The larger box Ask Extension mentions for Jersey Giants and Brahmas stops at “larger.”

A Tractor Supply retailer guide, “Chicken Nesting Boxes Know-How,” does print three sizes. The PDF does not give an author or a date. It is a retailer’s guide, not an extension bulletin. It says a good nest for Leghorns and other lightweight layers is 12 inches wide by 14 inches high by 12 inches deep. Heavier breeds, it says, need 14 inches wide by 14 inches high by 12 inches deep. For bantams it calls 10 inches wide by 12 inches high by 10 inches deep a reasonable size. Fourteen inches is 35.56 centimetres. Ten inches is 25.4 centimetres. The same guide says a nest that is slightly large is better than one that is too small and cramped, because a cramped nest can break eggs. Those three sizes are that guide’s sizes. They are not the missing Jersey Giant measurement from the Ask Extension answer.

How high to mount them

Height is where the pages spread out. The figures below are the ones they print. They are not combined into a single mounting height.

  • Oregon State says to avoid placing nesting boxes directly on the ground or lower than the roosting poles, and that this helps keep the boxes cleaner. The same page says, in general, to set roosting poles 2 feet off the floor, and to set Silkie poles 1 foot off the ground. It does not write those roost heights into the nest sentence. Two feet is 24 inches, and 24 × 2.54 = 60.96 centimetres. One foot is 12 inches, 30.48 centimetres.
  • Florida says to place the boxes about two feet above the ground. The “about” stays with that sentence. The conversion of two feet is the 60.96 centimetres above.
  • Maryland says roosts and nest boxes should be placed 24 inches above the floor, which is the same 60.96 centimetres, and that nests and roosts should be separated so the birds will not roost in the nest boxes. The page cites Clauer, 1997, for that separation. This page did not open the 1997 publication. Maryland also says to provide six inches of roost space per bird. Six inches is 15.24 centimetres.
  • Jacob’s page says to locate the nests at least 2 feet off the ground and at least 4 feet away from the roosts. Four feet is 48 inches, and 48 × 2.54 = 121.92 centimetres.
  • Kansas State says the boxes should be at least 2 feet off the ground, in the darker area of the facility, designed to discourage roosting, with sloping tops, and placed well away from any roof or overhang. It does not print the angle of the slope.
  • The Tractor Supply retailer guide says to place nests at floor level at first, for pullets just starting to lay, because floor eggs are most likely before the pullets have learned where the boxes are. After they learn, it says to raise the nests at least 18 inches off the floor. Eighteen inches times 2.54 is 45.72 centimetres. It says chickens are less likely to play in raised nests, which can soil and break eggs. Nests may be higher than 18 inches, for instance a second tier, and then it says to supply a simple ladder.

The retailer guide is the page in this set that says to start on the floor and raise the boxes later. Oregon State says to avoid the ground. Florida, Jacob, Kansas State, and Maryland give a height around two feet, or 24 inches, for the boxes themselves. Those instructions are left side by side.

A landing place in front of the box is on three of the pages, with different wording. Oregon State says that for boxes off the ground, place a pole on the outside of each box or across a row, so birds can jump and land there, and that a keeper might need a chicken-size, ladder-like ramp on a slant. Florida says a perch may be placed in front of each box, as a spot to land before entering. The retailer guide says that once the nests are off the floor, install a perch in front of the openings, far enough away that a hen resting on it cannot soil the nest. For most breeds, it says, the perch should be no closer than 8 inches from the opening. Eight inches is 20.32 centimetres.

Roosting on top of the boxes is a separate problem from sleeping inside them. Oregon State says a roof sloped at 45 degrees prevents birds from roosting on top of the boxes. Kansas State says sloping tops, with no angle. The 45-degree roof is the numbered slope in this set.

Why the eggs all end up in one box

The pages that address a shared box give a habit and a set of changes. They do not give one cause that replaces the others.

The Ask Extension answer says hens like to lay where other eggs already are. Its suggestion is a fake egg, in plastic, ceramic, rubber, or wood. It says some people have had success with rocks or golf balls. It also says the boxes should be in a darker, quieter part of the coop, because hens usually will not lay in bright, high-traffic areas, and that a box the hens are already using in the wrong place should be blocked off or made less attractive. Florida’s collecting section says ceramic or wooden eggs can be used to encourage hens to use the nesting boxes. That is the same kind of hint, limited to ceramic or wood on Florida’s page.

Kansas State’s EP-70 says that if too many hens begin laying in the same nest, try relocating the nest or moving the nesting material around. The reason it gives is fewer eggs in that one nest, and fewer broken eggs. It also says to remove eggs from the nest as often as possible. It does not print a number of times per day. Florida says to check the boxes at least once a day, and that eggs should not be allowed to accumulate, or the hens go out of production and want to sit on the eggs. It says the longer an egg stays in the nest, the more likely it is to become dirty, broken, or low quality, and it says to clean the nesting boxes once a week and replace soiled litter. Maryland says collecting twice a day will help maximize production. Once a day, twice a day, and as often as possible are three instructions. They are not averaged here.

Dark and quiet is on more than one page, for more than one reason. The New Hampshire and Connecticut sheet wants the inside of the box fairly private and dark, in its pecking list, and it says high light intensity and continuous lighting cause stress that can lead to pecking. Kansas State says to put the boxes in the darker area, and to reduce the light reaching the nesting area because that reduces pecking. Jacob’s page says not to use bright lights in the coop, especially near the nesting area, because bright light increases nervousness and picking. The RSPCA commercial standard says nest-box lighting must be used only in the morning, and only during initial nest-box training or temporarily to keep nest-box use even. A note under that standard says none or limited use of nest-box lighting has been shown to help minimise the risk of injurious feather pecking. The retailer guide says hens choose a darkened, secluded place, and that a flap of fabric can shield a box from bright light if needed. That instinct wording is the retailer guide’s. The extension pages above state a preference for a darker box, and they tie bright light near the nests to pecking.

Jacob’s page adds three nest details that change how crowded a box gets. Train pullets to use the nests before they begin laying, and provide roosts during the growing period to encourage nest use. Keep 2 inches of clean, dry nesting material in the nests at all times, as padding. Two inches is 5.08 centimetres. Remove broody hens from the nesting area, because they reduce nesting space and cause more traffic in the remaining nests. Do not scare hens out of the boxes. The page says the sudden movement can break eggs. Kansas State says to keep several inches of clean, dry nesting material in each box, names wheat straw as an excellent material, and says material contaminated with broken eggs should be removed and replaced. “Several inches” is the phrase that page prints. It is not converted to one number here. Ask Extension’s bedding list is pine shavings, a nest pad, or straw, kept clean. Maryland says always to provide clean, dry nesting material. The RSPCA commercial standard requires a floor substrate that encourages nesting, is managed so parasites and disease do not build up, and is not wire or plastic-coated wire that the birds can touch. The examples it gives are clean straw, Astro Turf, or dimpled rubber mats.

The retailer guide’s account of a shared box is about broken eggs. It says some hens will pile into a nest large enough for more than one hen, and that a nest big enough for more than one hen at a time leads to broken eggs. It says a hen that has to wait her turn may lay on the floor, which is why it says to offer one nest for every four hens, and at least two nests if the flock has four hens or fewer. That “four or fewer” floor is the retailer guide’s. It is not the same sentence as Ask Extension’s “at least two even for a small flock,” which is what the table applies, including at five hens where one-per-five rounds up to 1.

Eggs laid in a hiding place, and a hen that stops laying, are a different checklist. That list is on why chickens stop laying.

Hens sleeping in the nest boxes

Sleeping inside the box and roosting on the lid are both in these pages. The fixes are not the same sentence.

Maryland says to separate nests and roosts so the birds will not roost in the nest boxes. It says that when they do, the boxes become very dirty. If the layers insist on roosting in the boxes, it says to block the boxes off after the hens have laid for the day, until they understand. On that page, both the roosts and the nest boxes are placed 24 inches above the floor. The separation it asks for is not a difference in height.

Oregon State’s height rule points the other way: do not put the boxes on the ground or lower than the roosting poles, so they stay cleaner. Its 45-degree roof is about birds sitting on top of the boxes, not about birds sleeping inside them. Kansas State’s sloping tops, set well away from a roof or overhang, are also about discouraging roosting on the boxes. Jacob’s page does not describe birds sleeping in the nests. It does say to put the nests at least 4 feet, 121.92 centimetres, away from the roosts.

The retailer guide’s 18-inch raise, after the pullets have learned the nests, is about birds playing in the boxes and soiling or breaking eggs. That is the reason that guide prints. It is not Maryland’s instruction to close the boxes after laying.

Fixes the pages actually print

Each line is one page’s instruction. Where two lines disagree, both stay.

  • Provide about one box per four birds (Oregon State), at least one per five hens (Florida, and the New Hampshire and Connecticut sheet), or one per four or five with at least two boxes (Ask Extension). The table is that arithmetic for 2 to 20 hens. Jacob’s page adds a floor of six boxes.
  • Have the boxes in place by 18 to 20 weeks (Florida). Train pullets to the nests before they lay (Jacob). The retailer guide says to start the boxes on the floor for those pullets, then raise them at least 18 inches, 45.72 centimetres.
  • Use a box about 12 by 12 by 12 inches, 30.48 centimetres each way, on the pages that print that cube. Maryland prints 10 by 10 inches, 25.4 centimetres. Ask Extension says a larger box for Jersey Giants and Brahmas and does not say how much larger. The retailer guide’s lightweight, heavier, and bantam sizes are in the size section, labeled as that guide.
  • Keep the box dark, private, and quiet, and keep bright light away from it. The pages that say so are listed under the shared-box section, including the commercial limit on nest-box lighting.
  • Keep clean, soft bedding in the box: straw or shavings, half filling the box on Florida’s page; 2 inches, 5.08 centimetres, on Jacob’s page; several inches, with wheat straw named, on the Kansas State page.
  • Put a landing pole or perch in front of a raised box (Oregon State and Florida). The retailer guide says to keep that perch at least 8 inches, 20.32 centimetres, from the opening. Oregon State says a ramp might be needed. The retailer guide says a ladder for a tier above the first.
  • Slope the top so birds do not roost on the boxes: 45 degrees on Oregon State’s page, sloping tops with no angle on the Kansas State page.
  • If many hens use one box, add a fake egg (Ask Extension; Florida limits the examples to ceramic or wood), block the spot they should not use (Ask Extension), or move that nest or its bedding (Kansas State).
  • Collect often enough that eggs do not pile up: at least daily, and clean the boxes weekly (Florida); twice a day (Maryland); as often as possible (Kansas State).
  • If hens sleep in the boxes, separate the nests from the roosts and, if they insist, close the boxes after laying (Maryland). Oregon State’s instruction is the other height sentence: keep the boxes off the ground and no lower than the roosts, so the boxes stay cleaner.

What this page does not decide

It does not choose one box per four over one box per five. Both are printed, and the table shows both. It does not apply Jacob’s floor of six boxes to the table. It does not treat the RSPCA’s July 2025 standards as a rule for a backyard coop. Those standards are the commercial scheme, including the group-nest option of 1 square metre of nest substrate per 120 hens. The RSPCA’s pet-chicken advice is a different page and is cited on the space guide, not here.

It does not invent a measurement for a Jersey Giant or a Brahma. It does not turn the retailer guide’s bantam and heavy-breed sizes into university figures. It does not size the coop floor or the run. It does not say how many hens to keep for a household’s eggs. Eggs that never reach a box, and the reasons laying stops, stay on why chickens stop laying.

It is not a local law, and it is not veterinary advice. A city can restrict poultry, or the number of birds, without mentioning nest boxes. Nothing on this page is a count of boxes in coops this site has built.
