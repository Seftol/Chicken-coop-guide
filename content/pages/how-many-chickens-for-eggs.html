How many chickens do I need for eggs? A calculator with sources

A family of four does not have one right flock size. Three guides publish three different rules, and a hen in her first year lays more eggs than the same hen a year later. Use the row that matches how many eggs you want, then the calculator if your basket is different. The tables further down are that sum for one egg per person per day.

What the guides say
Source What it says
UNH Extension, July 2025 Around 4 to 6 chickens for a family of four. More if you eat a lot of eggs or give some away.
University of Queensland, 8 April 2022 About 4 or 5 chickens for around 20 to 25 eggs a week. Each hen lays 4 to 5 eggs a week, on average.
NMSU, Circular 477 For one egg per person per day, raise 12 chicks and keep 3 layers per family member. Under most circumstances there is little advantage in more than 18 to 20 layers, which should supply a family of 5 or 6 at that rate.

The RSPCA in the UK recommends at least three hens that get on well. Virginia Cooperative Extension says chickens need the company of at least two other chickens, so keep three even when the egg sum is lower, and hens do not need a rooster to lay eggs.

Why your hens lay less than the first-year rate

A breed’s yearly egg number is a first-year kind of figure. Age, day length, and a late start all shrink the basket. The fuller checklist, including molt, feed, and water, is on why chickens stop laying.

Age

  • UF/IFAS: about 65% after 12 months of lay. Good layers lay about 50 to 60 weeks, then molt. After two or three years many hens decline significantly.
  • UNH Extension: 10 hens lay about 9 eggs a day at first, then 6 or 7 a day after 13 months.
  • NC State: 12 hens average 9 or 10 eggs a day for several months, peak at 32 to 34 weeks of age, and may be down to 6 a day by the 12th month after laying begins.

Daylight

  • NC State: 14 to 16 hours of light in fall and winter to maximise laying. Resting hens through the short days is reasonable, and a shortening day often brings a molt and a stop of about two months.
  • UNH fact sheet: at least 14 hours of total light from 1 September to 1 May if you light the coop. That interferes with the natural molt.
  • UNH podcast: 12 to 14 hours a day. The same conversation describes a light on for 12 hours.

When the first egg arrives

  • NC State: pullets begin at 16 to 24 weeks.
  • UF/IFAS: about 18 to 22 weeks, then the peak 6 to 8 weeks later.
  • NMSU: pullets start at 20 to 24 weeks.
  • Virginia Tech: chicks take 4 to 6 months to lay.
  • UNH: around four months of age, with a five-month growing period, and five-month-old pullets may be ready to lay. Chicks bought in April or May lay around July or August in that northern-hemisphere example, not in Australia, where the short days fall around June.

Breed changes the number

The RSPCA lists annual eggs for four common UK layers: Gingernut Ranger 300, New Hampshire Red 280, Rhode Island Red 260, and Sussex and Sussex bantam 260. It marks the Rhode Island Red and the Sussex as dual purpose. The UNH podcast says chickens lay about 250 to 300 eggs a year on average, and that blue-egg and green-egg breeds lay fewer than some brown-egg and white-egg layers. The UNH fact sheet lists a Rhode Island Red at 250 to 280 eggs a year, beside the RSPCA’s 260; its other breed ranges are on the stop-laying page. For heritage breeds, use The Livestock Conservancy comparison chart. NC State suggests those breeds when slightly less than one egg per hen per day in the first year is enough and you want hens that keep laying longer.

Then size the coop

More hens need more floor, run, roost, and nest space, and those allowances disagree too. Once you have a count, use the reference chart for 2 to 20 standard hens and the calculator for the other cases.
