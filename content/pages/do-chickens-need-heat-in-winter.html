Do chickens need a heat lamp in winter?

Published extension guidance does not give one answer. The University of Minnesota Extension tells keepers to add heat when the coop falls below 35°F. The University of Maryland Extension says adult birds protected from wind and wet weather should not need any additional heat, even in the coldest part of the year. Ohio State University Extension says supplemental heat may or may not be necessary, and that cold-tolerant, acclimated birds in an insulated, dry, ventilated coop do not usually need it. Oregon State University Extension does not set a temperature for a heater. It says a coop does not have to be insulated, and it does have to be free of drafts.

This page puts those instructions side by side. It does not average them into a temperature nobody printed. It is general guidance from public pages, not veterinary advice. This site does not keep chickens, and it has not heated a coop or tested a lamp. Temperatures are given in degrees Fahrenheit, as the pages print them, and in degrees Celsius. Lengths are the inch figures those pages print, multiplied by 2.54 to give centimetres.

Short answer. For a healthy, fully feathered adult hen that has acclimated, in a coop that is dry, draft-free, and still ventilated, Maryland and Ohio say added heat is usually unnecessary. Minnesota says to add it when the coop itself, not the outdoor forecast, is below 35°F. That is 1.7°C to one decimal place, because (35 − 32) × 5/9 = 1.666…, and it is 2°C to the nearest degree. Young birds are the case these pages treat as needing heat. Molting hens, bantams, and birds with less feather or large combs are the other cases they single out, and those sentences are not all instructions to switch on a lamp.

What the sources say

The four extension pages below are the ones that answer the winter-heat question directly. Two fire-safety pages are included later, because they describe heat lamps and do not say whether a flock needs one. A threshold cell that says “none printed” means that page does not give a number.

Minnesota: a coop temperature, and signs the birds are cold

Colleen Carlson and Hannah Lochner put the heat instruction in the quick facts and again in the coop section: provide supplemental heat when coop temperatures fall below 35°F. They say to place that heat at the height of the nest boxes or the lower rung of the roost, and to put a thermometer on the wall at each of those heights. The 35°F line is a reading inside the coop at bird level. The page does not convert an outdoor forecast into that reading.

On the bird itself, the same page says most poultry can maintain body temperature when the environment is between 60 and 75°F. 60°F is 15.6°C and 75°F is 23.9°C, each rounded to one decimal place from (60 − 32) × 5/9 and (75 − 32) × 5/9. It says the average body temperature of a chicken is 106°F, which is 41.1°C to one decimal place. Cold stress, on that page, is what happens when the bird loses more heat than it produces, so its body temperature falls. The signs it names are huddling, holding a foot up to the breast, and puffed feathers. It says prolonged cold stress can reduce performance and can lead to death.

The heaters it names are radiant sources that heat the birds rather than the air: brooder plates, panels, and hanging heaters, approved for chickens and livestock, with the fire warnings and the installation instructions read. A thermostat is described as a way to manage that use. An approved heat lamp with a heat bulb is “another option,” with the mounting rules in the safety section. Heavier breeds it says over-winter well are Plymouth Rock, Wyandotte, Ameraucana, and Orpington. Smaller breeds, birds with less feathering, and birds with large combs and wattles “may need extra care.” The page does not define that extra care as a lamp.

Maryland: adults without wind and wet, and the exceptions

FS-1133, by Jon Moyle, Maegan Perdue, Emily Lhaman, and Jennifer Timmons, splits the flock by age and by condition. Young birds “will need a source of heat to survive and grow.” Adult birds protected from wind and wet weather “should not need any additional heat even during the coldest time of year.” The same paragraph says adding heat to older birds is not necessary, and that it will help them maintain egg production through the winter. Hens that are molting “may need some extra heat (and or protection)” until the feathers grow back. The parentheses are the page’s: heat, or protection, or both.

Bantams, that page says, do not produce the same amount of heat as large fowl, so bantams may need supplemental heat while large fowl look comfortable. Large combs are a frostbite note, not a heat-lamp note: Leghorns, Minorcas, and other Mediterranean breeds are the birds it names, and the steps it names are petroleum jelly on the comb and wattles, or a baby sock over the comb. Propane and kerosene heaters are discussed only as a fire hazard. The alternatives it names are heating lamps, bulbs, or other radiant heaters that have automatic shutoffs, with the warning that a heating lamp can still start a fire if it is not secured.

Ohio: acclimate, then decide

Sabrina Schirtzinger and Tim McDermott open the heating section with “may or may not be necessary.” They say chickens can withstand substantial cold if drafts and excess humidity are gone and the birds can find a warm, dry spot. They say to let the flock acclimate as winter approaches, which builds tolerance. Insulation, on this page, can hold warmth without electricity, and the insulation must be out of the birds’ reach. The sentence that says heat is usually unnecessary has five conditions in it: a cold-tolerant breed, birds that have acclimated, an insulated coop, a dry coop, and adequate ventilation.

The cold-tolerant breeds that page lists are Ameraucanas, Ancona, Black Australorps, Black Giant, Brahma, Buff Orpingtons, Cochins, Delaware, Dominique, Langshan, New Hampshire, Plymouth Rocks, Rhode Island Red, Speckled Sussex, and Wyandottes. It then says a large comb or wattle, including on roosters of most breeds, can still be where frostbite starts. Earlier, the ventilation section says most breeds can tolerate cold, even sub-freezing temperatures, and that wind and moisture together with cold are when complications arise. If heat is used, the electrical feed has to be sturdy, safe, and inaccessible to the chickens, and space heaters, radiant heating, and heat lamps are all flagged for excess heat, carbon dioxide buildup, and fire.

Oregon: ventilation and drafts, not a heater schedule

EC 1644 is a coop-design publication. Its ventilation section says ventilation removes ammonia, carbon dioxide, and moisture, and brings in fresh air. A well-designed coop has adequate air exchange without drafts. “Coops do not need to be insulated, but be sure there are no drafts.” Passive ventilation, through windows or through vents in gables, eaves, or the roof, is described as enough for most backyard coops. Fans, if they are used, should not blow on the birds. In the maintenance section the same publication says to install electric lights, and that those lights also serve as a heat source during extremely cold weather. That sentence is about the coop’s lights. It is not a heat-lamp specification, and it does not name a temperature.

Floor area, roost length, and nest counts from this publication are already on the space guide. They are not repeated here except where a winter page adds a perch detail that is about cold feet.

When heat is what a page calls for

Each item is a condition a cited page attaches to heat, to extra care, or to a bird that may not cope. They are not a single rule, and they are not a reason to leave a lamp on all winter “just in case.”

  • The coop is below 35°F (1.7°C) at nest height or the lower roost. That is Minnesota’s instruction, including in a coop that is otherwise prepared for winter. Maryland’s no-heat sentence and Ohio’s “do not usually” sentence do not use this number. A dry, draft-free coop below 35°F is exactly where those pages disagree.
  • The birds are young. Maryland says young birds need a source of heat to survive and grow. The National Fire Protection Association’s February 2023 article, cited in the safety section, says chicks cannot regulate body temperature for the first few weeks, so supplemental heat is necessary: as high as 95°F in the first week, then down to about 65°F over the following weeks. 95°F is 35°C, because (95 − 32) × 5/9 = 35. About 65°F is 18.3°C when (65 − 32) × 5/9 is rounded to one decimal place. Those are brooder temperatures for chicks. They are not a target for a coop of adult hens, and Maryland does not print them.
  • A hen is molting. Maryland says she may need extra heat, or extra protection, or both, until the feathers return. Protection without a heater is one of the options that sentence allows. A molt is also why laying stops in winter. The checklist for that, including day length, is on why chickens stop laying.
  • The birds are bantams. Maryland says bantams produce less heat than large fowl and may need supplemental heat while the large fowl look comfortable. It does not give a temperature for that difference.
  • The birds are small, lightly feathered, or large-combed. Minnesota says those birds may need extra care to stay warm, hold body weight, and stay healthy. Ohio says a large comb or wattle can frostbite on an otherwise cold-tolerant breed. Maryland’s large-comb steps are petroleum jelly, which it says protects the comb, and a sock. None of those three sentences says the extra care is a heat lamp.
  • The bird is old, light, or unwell. Minnesota says old age, low body weight, or poor health can keep a bird from adapting, and that prolonged cold stress can end in death. The step that page names for birds in that condition is to consider culling before winter, as the humane option it is willing to state. It says to consult a veterinarian for frostbite or an infection. It does not say to hang a lamp over a sick bird instead. This page does not add a culling method or a treatment.
  • You are heating in order to keep eggs coming. Maryland says heat for older birds is not necessary and that it helps them keep laying through winter. Ohio says a winter drop in laying is natural because chickens require 14 hours of daylight, and that 12 to 14 hours of light will help production. Minnesota’s cold-weather page says about 14 hours of light for optimal production, added in the morning. A smaller basket in winter is the subject of the laying checklist. How many hens the household’s egg use implies, at a first-year rate and at a later rate, is on how many chickens for eggs. A heater is not one of the inputs on that page.

Cold stress and frostbite

Minnesota’s cold-stress signs are huddling, holding a foot up to the breast, and puffed feathers. The page says those signs mean the chickens may be cold, and that a bird in cold stress is losing more heat than it can produce.

Minnesota says frostbite on the comb, wattles, and feet may turn the tissue black or gray, and that the tissue may feel brittle. The causes it names are high moisture and cold together, and it says severe frostbite can mean loss of the comb, wattle, or foot. Ohio says the injury is most common on the feet, combs, and wattles, that the cells freeze, and that gray, black, or brittle areas are the indicators. It says an extreme case can mean the loss of the extremity. Ohio also says humid air creates more frostbite risk than drier air, and that ammonia from litter in a tightly sealed coop can lead to respiratory disease. Minnesota says to consult a veterinarian for frostbite or an infection. Ohio describes moving the bird to a warm space and warming the tissue slowly, says not to warm it rapidly, and says a warm-water soak is for feet and not for the comb or wattle. That is Ohio’s description. It is not a protocol this site has used, and it is not a substitute for a veterinarian.

Minnesota says petroleum jelly on the comb and wattles can help insulate them and prevent frostbite damage, and that petroleum jelly does not treat frostbite once it is there. Maryland also says to coat combs and wattles with petroleum jelly. Neither page prints a brand.

What the pages say to do before adding a heater

Maryland’s no-heat sentence and Ohio’s “do not usually” sentence both depend on the coop. A wet coop, or a draft on the birds, is outside those sentences. The work those pages describe first is to fix that, which is also what Minnesota pairs with its heater instruction.

Dry air and dry bedding

Maryland says to fix a leaking roof before winter, because moisture raises ammonia and damp air feels much colder than dry air. It says to close cracks and holes that let wind blow on the birds. Minnesota says chicken manure is 70 percent water, so manure under the roosts and on the floor is a moisture problem, and that moisture plus cold can condense and lead to frostbite. If the coop smells of ammonia, or moisture is collecting on objects or windows, that page says to increase ventilation and clean up the manure.

Bedding depths are not the same number. Minnesota describes a deep bed of 4 to 6 inches of straw or shavings. Four inches is 10.16 centimetres (4 × 2.54) and 6 inches is 15.24 centimetres (6 × 2.54). The method it describes is to stir the bed so manure moves down, then add fresh bedding on top, and it says this produces some heat as the manure breaks down. Maryland describes deep litter as insulation from the ground and as heat from microbial breakdown, at 3 to 4 inches of pine shavings or straw. Three inches is 7.62 centimetres (3 × 2.54). Ohio does not print a depth. It says to remove the old bedding, clean, and put in a large amount of new dry bedding, which keeps the flock warm by insulation. The materials it names are wood shavings or chips, straw, soft hay, ground corn cobs, or shredded paper. It also says deep bedding can raise humidity, so the moisture has to be checked, and the bed fluffed, more often. The 3-to-4-inch figure and the 4-to-6-inch figure both stand. This page does not pick a depth between them.

Drafts at bird level, openings up high

All four extension pages want fresh air and do not want that air blowing on the birds. Maryland says incoming air should mix with the warmer air already in the building before it reaches the birds, and that a vent or opening near the roof or the ceiling lets ammonia and stale air out. Minnesota’s examples are a partly open south-facing window, roof vents, burlap over an open window, and a small opening on a milder winter day. Ohio says to seal doors, windows, and holes, including with a tarp over an open-sided coop, and to leave ventilation in place so humidity and ammonia do not build up. Its tarp instruction is to keep the covering from running all the way to the roof, so the top of the coop can still exhaust moisture. Oregon says the exchange has to be real and the coop still draft-free, and that insulation is not required for that.

Insulation is a separate disagreement. Oregon says coops do not need it. Maryland says it is not necessary, that it keeps the birds’ own heat in, and that it is useful if you are already adding heat. Ohio says it can hold warmth without electricity. Minnesota says to consider insulating an unfinished coop, with the insulation closed off so birds cannot peck it and rodents cannot live in it. Those are four instructions. They are not combined here into “insulate” or “do not insulate.”

A wooden roost the feet can cover

Minnesota says roosts should start at least 12 inches above the floor, 30.48 centimetres, with 9 inches of room per chicken, 22.86 centimetres, so the birds can fluff their feathers and sit together without crowding. While they roost they lie on their feet. It says to avoid metal, plastic, and other materials that stay cold and can cause frostbite, and that wooden 2-by-4 or 2-by-2 boards work. The page states those as 2-by-4-inch and 2-by-2-inch boards: 2 inches is 5.08 centimetres and 4 inches is 10.16 centimetres. Maryland says the same thing about metal and plastic pipe, and it prefers a flat perch such as a 2-by-4 so the feathers can cover the feet. It also says not to put perches over feed or water. Ohio says every bird should be able to roost off the floor, with at least 6 to 8 inches of roost space each, which is 15.24 to 20.32 centimetres, on a roost 1½ to 3 inches across, 3.81 to 7.62 centimetres. Oregon’s design figure is different again: generally 2 feet off the floor, which is 60.96 centimetres, on 1- to 2-inch square wood with the top edges rounded, and 5 to 10 inches of pole per bird. Silkies, on that page, are an exception at 1 foot, 30.48 centimetres. Nine inches, 6 to 8 inches, and 5 to 10 inches are three published lengths. The space guide already lines the Minnesota and Oregon lengths up with the other housing pages. Ohio’s 6 to 8 inches is this fact sheet’s figure. It is not folded into those.

Crowding sits next to roost length because a bird that cannot get on the roost is standing on the cold floor. Minnesota says heavier or more dominant birds may keep others off the feed, the water, or the roost, and that a mixed flock may need to be separated by size or by dominance in winter. Floor area for the hours the birds spend shut in is the indoors question on the space guide, which uses Oregon’s higher indoor allowance when there is no outdoor access. This page does not invent a winter floor area. Minnesota does not print one either.

Water that is still liquid

Minnesota says that without water, chickens stop eating, and that the water has to be kept from freezing. The devices it names are a plastic heated fount, a heated base under a metal fount, and a heated dog dish that is partly covered so birds do not step in or drown. It says to set the water up on a stand so birds do not scratch manure and bedding into it, and so the heating element stays out of the bedding. Maryland says water intake usually falls in winter and the birds still need water, that they cannot break ice, and that the water should be checked and replaced several times a day. A heater under the waterer is the electrical option it describes. A heat lamp aimed at the water is the other, with two warnings: cold water splashed on a hot bulb can shatter it, and the heated base must not melt the drinker or start a fire if it touches litter. Ohio says to change the water twice a day and more often on colder days, to check it in the evening, and that even an automatic waterer freezes if it is not heated or insulated. It says heated bowls can malfunction and cause a fire. No product on those lists is linked here.

Feed, including the disagreement about how much

Minnesota says a balanced diet stays in place all winter, that intake may rise by up to 25 percent in cold weather, and that scratch should stay at no more than a handful per 10 birds. It says digestion of scratch produces heat, so a handful before roosting can help on a cold night, and that treats must not replace the ration. Maryland says feed should be available at all times because cold weather takes more energy, and that energy-dense treats and scratch in the evening help keep birds warm overnight. In that same note it says to feed suet cakes sparingly and to limit that extra food to 10 percent of total intake. Ohio says chickens will eat less in winter than in summer, that each laying hen requires 2 pounds of feed a week, and that a small amount of scratch late in the day supplies carbohydrate for warmth and for eggs. Two pounds is 0.91 kilograms to two decimal places, because 2 × 0.45359237 = 0.90718474, using the international avoirdupois pound of 0.45359237 kilograms. “Up to 25 percent more” and “less than in summer” are both printed. This page does not reconcile them, and it does not turn a handful and 10 percent into one scoop.

Heat lamps, heaters, and fire

If the conditions above are the reason you add heat, every page that discusses the equipment treats fire as part of the decision. None of them names a product to buy.

  • Minnesota. Do not hang a heat lamp by its cord. A cage around the bulb is there so a fall does not put the bulb in the bedding. Keep wires away from the birds, the water, and flammable litter. Extension cords are short-term only, and they are not how power should be run to the coop. Electricity in the coop is a job for a professional electrician. Read the fire warnings and follow the installation instructions. The radiant heaters it lists, plates, panels, and hanging heaters, are the option that heats birds rather than the whole air space, and the product has to be approved for chickens and livestock.
  • Maryland. Propane and kerosene heaters are a fire hazard in a building of wood, bedding, and feathers. The alternatives it names are heating lamps, bulbs, or other radiant heaters that shut off automatically. Even a heating lamp can cause a fire if it is not secured. Do not splash cold water on a hot lamp.
  • Ohio. The electrical supply has to be sturdy, safe, and out of the chickens’ reach. Space heaters, radiant heating, and heat lamps can produce too much heat, a buildup of carbon dioxide, or a fire. Heated water bowls can malfunction and start a fire. Leaks from waterers add the humidity the frostbite section is trying to avoid.
  • National Fire Protection Association, 8 February 2023. Corey Hannahs’s article says heat lamps, heated waterers, and space heaters are used both to keep water from freezing and to warm chicks, and that hay, bedding, and other combustibles next to those heat sources are a serious fire hazard. It says chicks need supplemental heat for the first weeks of life, at the brooder temperatures quoted above, which is why a brooder is a different problem from an adult winter coop. The dangerous setup it describes is a DIY brooder that puts a heat lamp on a cardboard or plastic box filled with pine shavings, inside a house. Electrical work, it says, belongs to a qualified electrician. Plan receptacles so the coop does not depend on extension cords. Use ground-fault protection because the building is damp. Use equipment that is listed by a qualified testing laboratory, follow the listing instructions, and do not use makeshift gear. Use dust-resistant boxes and fully enclosed lamps, and clean the dust off.
  • National Fire Protection Association tip sheet, © 2018. The Backyard Chicken Coop sheet says to secure heat lamps so they cannot be knocked over, to keep heat lamps and space heaters away from anything that can burn, and to put space heaters on a surface where they cannot tip. It says not to use extension cords in the coop, to use equipment made for agricultural or commercial use, to have a qualified electrician do the wiring, to check wiring for damage, to cover bulbs so dirt, moisture, and breakage are less likely, to brush cobwebs and dust off fixtures and outlets, to ground the cord and plug of an electrically heated waterer, and to use outlets and switch boxes that keep out dust and water. It also says to store baled hay away from the livestock and away from anything that can burn.
  • Clark County Fire Marshal, 6 May 2021. Clark County’s notice says multiple recent fires in the county started from heat lamps used to keep livestock or pets warm. Fire Marshal Dan Young’s sentence on that page is: “Many fires can be prevented by using common safety measures and knowing the risks of equipment that can be hazardous when used incorrectly.” The page then lists tips it attributes to the National Fire Protection Association, including one the 2018 sheet above does not word the same way: a spring clamp by itself is not enough to keep a heat lamp from coming loose. The 2018 sheet says to secure the lamp so it will not be knocked over, and it does not mention a spring clamp. Both documents say to keep the lamp away from anything that can burn, and both say not to run the coop on extension cords.

An egg-laying light is a different device from a heat lamp. Penn State and the University of Arizona say so on the pages already cited in the bulb section of the laying checklist, including Arizona’s statement that heat lamps are a serious fire risk and should be avoided. Those sentences are about the bulb used to lengthen the day. They are not repeated in full here.

What this page does not decide

It does not choose Minnesota’s 35°F over Maryland’s “even during the coldest time of year,” or over Ohio’s “do not usually.” All three are printed, and they apply to different conditions. It does not turn 35°F into an outdoor temperature. It does not apply the NFPA brooder figures, 95°F and about 65°F, to adult hens. It does not treat a winter drop in eggs as proof the coop is too cold. Day length and molt are on the laying checklist, and the hen count for a household’s eggs is on how many chickens for eggs.

It does not size a coop. Birds that stay inside through a cold spell still need the floor, roost, and nest space on the space guide. It does not say that a United States extension bulletin is the rule in the United Kingdom, the European Union, Australia, or any particular city. Oregon’s “coops do not need to be insulated” is that publication’s design statement, not a building code. It does not cover ducks, geese, or turkeys, which Maryland mentions only to say waterfowl and turkeys are naturally hardy and that ducks and geese still need water deep enough to dunk their heads.

It is not veterinary advice. Cold-stress signs, frostbite signs, and the instruction to call a veterinarian are here because those pages print them. Nothing on this page is a diagnosis, a dose, or a claim about one bird.
